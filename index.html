AI in Financial Policy

An analysis of policy attention and risk framing around artificial intelligence in international banking regulation and supervision. How attention has evolved, where and how risks are framed, and how the conversation has moved with the arrival of generative AI.

This demo is built on a long methodological tradition of policy analysis and quantitative text analysis. Systematic content analysis dates from the 1970s as a research method for coding documents at scale (Krippendorff 1980, Neuendorf 2002). Text-as-data approaches for policy analysis have developed since (Gritsenko and Mäkelä 2021, Grimmer, Roberts and Stewart 2022, Hassan et al. 2025), and recent advances in language models and topic modelling have made the tools that earlier theory just envisioned, accessible at scale. The analysis here combines the traditional methodologies with these modern instruments to produce results that are theory-grounded, reproducible, and auditable.

Scope and data collection

Methodology → Database architecture → Codebook →

387 documents were collected from five international bodies in financial regulation and supervision, covering 2018 to 2025. Each body's publications page was the primary source. The BIS, for instance, has an "AI at BIS" section, with equivalent AI-tagged collections at FATF, IMF, OECD, and the World Bank. A document was included if it mentioned "artificial intelligence" or "machine learning". Documents focused on topics outside the financial system, such as labour or health AI applications, were excluded.

The number of documents that mention artificial intelligence or machine learning grows over 2018 to 2025, with a sharp acceleration after ChatGPT's release. The three post-ChatGPT years account for roughly 70 % of the database, with 48, 86, and 112 documents in 2023, 2024, and 2025 respectively. Hovering on any bar reveals the per-body breakdown for that year.

Policy papers and academic papers each account for roughly a third of the database. Another 17 % consists of speeches and commentary, and the rest comes from stocktakes, formal standards, and country assessments. The supervisory work of these institutions and their internal research are receiving comparable attention to AI, though the content of that attention differ as shown next.

Trends in policy attention

Methodology →

Attention to AI in these documents did not begin with ChatGPT. Documents from 2019 to 2021 already discussed AI and machine learning, often using terms like neural networks, natural language processing, and deep learning. After ChatGPT, the discussion grew in volume and the vocabulary expanded. Generative AI, large language models, and agentic systems become standard terms in writing from 2023 onward. The interest is older than the language used to describe it.

1 — Supervised. Hand-coded methods

Traditional methods measure attention to a topic by counting how often relevant words appear. The approach requires human expertise to pick the vocabulary, since the count is only as good as the list. For this demo, twenty-three terms were chosen to cover the AI and machine learning vocabulary in use across the period. Each document is then summarised by the number of times these terms appear per thousand words, which makes documents of different length comparable.

Before ChatGPT, these terms appear at a modest rate. The dominant ones in pre-2022 writing are machine learning, neural networks, and natural language processing. From 2023 onward the rate rises sharply and remains at the higher level. The post-ChatGPT years bring new terms into common use, including generative AI, large language models, agentic systems, and ChatGPT itself.

2 — Semi-supervised. Semantic similarity method

Traditional methods are useful as a first measure but can mask real attention by depending entirely on a fixed list of words. A semi-supervised method based on transformer models compares each paragraph to a curated set of AI reference sentences and scores the similarity in meaning rather than wording. By this measure, attention to artificial intelligence and machine learning rises steadily across the period, without the sudden jump seen in the keyword count.

The shaded bands around the line are bootstrap confidence intervals which quantify how precise each year's estimate is. They also help test whether differences between periods are statistically meaningful, and whether topical dispersion narrows or widens over time. A preliminary look at this figure suggests the bands are tighter from 2023 onward, which might reflect closer semantic similarity in the topics discussed post-ChatGPT. The same overall conclusion holds whether the unit is the paragraph or the sentence.

τ is the minimum semantic similarity between a paragraph (or sentence) and the closest anchor. Different sensitivities suggest the same trend.

Attention landscape

Methodology →

The previous section traced a steady rise in attention to AI. The next question is where that attention sits, and how its composition has changed. In the years before ChatGPT, the AI policy agenda of these bodies was dominated by consumer protection, data protection, and the broader fintech transformation. After ChatGPT, the balance shifted toward capacity and governance, labour-market exposure, and operational concerns within banks and central banks. New categories also emerged that had no real presence before 2022, including generative AI as a distinct policy object and AI-related financial-system vulnerabilities. The ethics agenda has not disappeared, but it now sits inside a wider frame about how supervisors actually oversee AI use in financial institutions.

1 — Hand-coded method

Hand-coded methods measure attention by having experts read each document and tag the problems it discusses. It is the slowest method, but also the most accurate, since every label is set by a trained reader rather than an algorithm. For this demo, eleven problem categories were defined in advance and applied across the database. The figure shows the seven that draw the most attention, with the share of attention computed for the periods before and after ChatGPT.

The largest change is in consumer and data protection, which fell from roughly 29 % of attention before ChatGPT to 12 % after (strange but maybe is my expertise in coding these issues). Capacity and governance moved in the opposite direction, rising from 20 % to 32 % and becoming the top category. Job displacement also rose sharply, from 7 % to 19 %. Model risk, cyber security, and operational risk remained relatively stable.

2 — Semi-supervised method

Expert-coded methods, though robust, can mask topics that fall outside the categories the coder set in advance. A semi-supervised method addresses this by letting the data suggest groups of its own, while still using the hand-coded categories as a starting point. Each paragraph in the database is converted into a vector that captures its meaning, and paragraphs with semantic similarity end up close to one another. The clusters that emerge can then point a supervisor toward directions worth a closer look.

The figure shows categories the codebook did not anticipate. The most salient pattern is that AI itself becomes the object of governance discussions, separated from the broader fintech framing that contained it before 2022. Around it, supervisors begin to build new oversight infrastructure for measuring exposure, monitoring systemic risk, and reporting incidents. In summary, these point to AI becoming a regulated domain in its own right, rather than a sub-topic within general fintech discussions.

Other potential analysis

The previous sections measured where attention sits and how it has moved. Three directions extend the analysis naturally as next steps.

Risk vs tool

Financial policy documents treat AI on both sides of their agenda. The same body can frame AI as a source of risk and as a tool of policy, sometimes in the same document. The dual treatment shows up across concrete features of the technology.

Feature As tool (suptech / regtech) As risk
Anomaly detection in transactions and conduct AML and CFT flagging, market-manipulation surveillance, misleading-marketing detection False positives at scale, opaque flags, criminals using similar techniques to evade detection
Forecasting of financial-system distress Liquidity-distress and default models, GDP nowcasting, scenario design for resilience tests Correlated model errors across institutions, procyclical amplification, false confidence in tail-risk estimates
Regulatory text understanding Parsing board minutes, fit-and-proper questionnaires, regulatory filings, cross-jurisdictional rule comparison Misinterpretation of legal language, opacity of how a conclusion was reached
Customer-facing decision automation Underwriting, instant credit approval, personalised insurance pricing, robo-advice Discrimination, exclusion of thin-file applicants, unappealable decisions, lack of explanation to the customer

Each feature can be located on the risk-tool spectrum by tracking how regulators, researchers, and commentary frame it, both at a point in time and over the years. That exercise is in itself a form of policy benchmarking. Where the framings converge on a feature, principles and guidance are ripe to emerge. Where they stay dispersed, the question is still open and the field needs more research, discussion, and understanding.

Incorporating quantitative data

Analysis does not have to stay in the terrain of text as data. Modern methods are useful to conduct deeper statistical inference work that might help to robustify falsifiable claims mentioned in policy papers.

If it is possible to couple unstructured data with other quantitative data, cross-jurisdictional variation becomes a natural source of identification. Different jurisdictions may have adopted different stances on the same questions, at different points in time, and the supervisory outcomes that followed are potentially observable. Several FSI papers make potentially testable causal claims of this type. A claim taken from Pérez-Cruz et al., 2025.

Coming back to FinRep

In 2020 I contributed to the early stages of the BIS FinTech Repository (FinRep), which launched in March 2022 and provided centralised access to fintech-related policy and regulatory documents from BIS-member central banks and supervisors in 63 jurisdictions, alongside output from international standard-setting bodies. FinRep is now closing. The BIS's own closure note states that "recent advances in technology have made it easier to search, summarise and compare relevant regulations across jurisdictions and international bodies. As such, the need for FinRep is significantly diminished."

The same technical advances that justify the closure are the methods this demo uses. FinRep accumulated four years of cross-jurisdictional regulatory writing on fintech topics spanning CBDC, crypto-assets, open banking, suptech, big-tech-in-finance, and AI. Applying the methods shown above to that material would answer the questions FinRep's users came for, at scale and reproducibly. How have specific regulatory frameworks diffused across jurisdictions? Which bodies led and which followed? Where and when did consensus form on a given topic? Which approaches found adoption and which did not?

FinRep's closure leaves a structured, dated, cross-jurisdictional dataset, ready to be analysed. The same workflow this demo shows can be applied to it. Text-as-data measures of attention and framing across jurisdictions can be coupled with the supervisory and financial-soundness indicators FSI Senior Advisors already work with. The methodology shown here is the natural next step for making use of FinRep's accumulated content.
